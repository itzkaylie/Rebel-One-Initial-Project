Each lead is a hypothesis, not a finding. A high complaint count alone doesn't make something a business opportunity.

Keep in mind

  • Different places. Credit outcomes are by county; complaints are counted for the whole state. A state complaint pattern doesn't mean it happened in any particular county.
  • Side by side isn't cause and effect. Nothing here shows that one causes the other.
  • Complaints aren't a random sample. They come only from people who chose to file one, and bigger states have more complaints simply because they have more people. Complaints show frustration, not proven demand.
  • The credit data looks back. It describes people born 1978–1985, by the county where they grew up, measured in 2020 — not today's residents. Numbers for small counties are less certain.

Before treating a lead as an opportunity, check:

  1. Does it last? It should show up year after year, not in one spike.
  2. Does it affect enough people? Compare with the state's total and size.
  3. Do people want it solved? Look for people already spending time or money on a fix, and talk to 5–10 of them.

Where I'd look next: 211 community needs data

211 is a free, confidential helpline people contact to find local help with things like rent, food or utility bills. Some 211 organizations publish summaries of the kinds of help people ask for: totals by category and area, with no names or personal details. That could add a more direct signal of what families need right now, for needs such as housing, childcare, jobs, food and utilities. The Atlas looks back at long-run outcomes, and CFPB complaints show problems with financial products.

Alabama 211 Counts dashboard

Covers: Alabama.

What it shows: total calls and help requests, the top request categories (such as housing & shelter, utilities, food, employment & income, and child care & parenting), smaller subcategories like rent assistance, a share marked "unmet", a map by ZIP code, county or legislative district, and month-by-month counts.

Access: view it online. Its "About the data" notes don't mention a download or API, so this site links to it instead of importing it.

Its notes say one caller can make more than one request, and call centers code requests differently, so category totals don't always match.

211 Data Pulse (FAQ)

Covers: a pilot from United Way Worldwide. Its state list currently shows Alabama, Maine, Massachusetts, New Jersey, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, South Dakota, Virginia and Wisconsin. New York is not on the list.

What it shows: top needs, top referrals and top "gaps" (needs where no resource was found), with filters by state, county, ZIP code and more. Its FAQ says data is added monthly.

Access: view it online. The FAQ says it can't provide downloadable data in this early version, and it mentions no API.

The FAQ says clients' names aren't shared with it, counts under 10 are hidden, and a gap "is not a full measure of unresolved need."

211 New York

Covers: New York.

No public needs data found yet. The Community Needs Assessment page showed no assessment, data or report when checked, and 211 Data Pulse doesn't list New York. To get New York request data, ask 211 New York directly whether it publishes aggregated summaries.

Related, but not needs data: the 211 New York service search, run by New York State 211, is a directory of local services people can be referred to. It doesn't publish counts of requests, and it notes it is "still under construction."

What 211 data can and can't tell us

  • It shows what people who contacted 211 asked for. It isn't a survey of every family. People who don't know about 211, or who get help elsewhere, aren't counted.
  • A referral isn't a result. A referral count doesn't prove the person got help or that the need was solved.
  • Counts depend on who runs the line. Categories and coding differ between 211 centers, so compare places carefully.
  • Public summaries only. This project uses only aggregated, public summaries. It never requests, stores or shows private caller records, names, phone numbers or call notes.

How the leads are chosen

Simple, visible rules in lib/signals.js turn the loaded data into up to five possible leads: the most-reported complaint type, the fastest-growing one between the last two full years, the weakest counties, the spread between counties, and the credit measure next to the complaint category on the same topic. The page shows three different ones, in that preference order: growth, county spread, side-by-side, weakest counties, most-reported. Complaint types with fewer than 50 complaints and states with fewer than 5 counties with data are skipped.
