How to separate signal from noise

1. Is it persistent?

Check the same product + issue in each full year (use the year menu). A problem that shows up year after year is more meaningful than a one-year spike. Watch for CFPB renaming product categories, which can make counts jump or drop without any real change.

2. Does it affect enough people?

Compare a count to the state's total and to the state's population — big states have more complaints simply because they have more people. For Atlas values, look at the sample size (n) and standard error (SE) where available; small counties can swing a lot by chance.

3. Is it a problem people want solved?

Complaints show frustration, not demand. Look for evidence that people are already spending time or money on a fix (workarounds, paid services, switching providers), then talk to 5–10 people affected before treating it as an opportunity.

4. Remember who is (and isn't) counted

CFPB complaints come only from people who chose to file one — not a random sample. Atlas credit outcomes describe people born 1978–1985 by the county where they grew up, measured in 2020, not today's residents.
