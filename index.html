Three leads to investigate

Each lead is a hypothesis, not a finding. A high complaint count alone doesn't make something a business opportunity. These leads use the Atlas and CFPB data loaded into this site; 211 data can only be viewed on the official 211 dashboards, so check them yourself (below).

211: where people are asking for help

211 is a free, confidential helpline people contact to find local help with things like rent, food, utility bills, jobs or childcare. Some 211 organizations publish summaries of what people ask for: totals by need and area, with no names, phone numbers or call notes. It's the closest of the three sources to current community demand.

How to read 211 data

  • A request is a signal, not a survey. It shows what people who contacted 211 asked for, not what every family needs. People who don't know about 211, or who get help elsewhere, aren't counted.
  • A referral isn't a result. A referral count doesn't prove the person got help or that the need was solved. 211 Data Pulse's FAQ says a referral "does not indicate whether resources were utilized."
  • A "gap" is a partial picture. Data Pulse counts gaps when no resource could be found, but its FAQ says a gap "is not a full measure of unresolved need."
  • "No 211 data available" doesn't mean "no need." It only means the dashboards checked here don't include that place.
  • Counts depend on who runs the line. Categories and coding differ between 211 centers, and one person can ask about several needs.

Questions to bring to LoopedIn

  1. "Which needs come up repeatedly in your community?"
  2. "What happens when people are referred to existing services? Do they get help, or come back with the same need?"

Keep in mind

  • Different places and years. Atlas outcomes are by county and measured in 2020; CFPB complaints are counted for the whole state over the dates shown; 211 dashboards use their own areas and dates. A pattern in one doesn't mean it happened in the same place or time in another.
  • Side by side isn't cause and effect. Nothing here shows that one causes another.
  • Complaints and 211 requests aren't random samples. They come only from people who chose to file or call. Bigger states have more simply because they have more people. They show frustration or need, not proven demand for a product.
  • The credit data looks back. It describes people born 1978–1985, by the county where they grew up, measured in 2020, not today's residents. Numbers for small counties are less certain.

Before treating a lead as an opportunity, check:

  1. Does it last? It should show up year after year, not in one spike.
  2. Does it affect enough people? Compare with the state's total and size.
  3. Do people want it solved? Look for people already spending time or money on a fix, and talk to 5–10 of them.

How the leads are chosen

Simple, visible rules in lib/signals.js turn the loaded data into up to five possible leads: the most-reported complaint type, the fastest-growing one between the last two full years, the weakest counties, the spread between counties, and the credit measure next to the complaint category on the same topic. The page shows three different ones, in that preference order: growth, county spread, side-by-side, weakest counties, most-reported. Complaint types with fewer than 50 complaints and states with fewer than 5 counties with data are skipped.
